# Positioning

Status: Working draft  
Last reviewed: 2026-08-24

## Positioning decision

Position Time To Do as a **visual day planner for time-blind brains and plans that change**, not as a general to-do manager, calendar replacement, habit tracker, or AI scheduler.

The initial go-to-market wedge is Android: a platform with broad planner demand but without an observed Android release from the closest task-native radial competitors, Dialed and Weel. Android is the launch advantage, not the permanent identity; the underlying promise should travel to other platforms later.

The category phrase should remain literal enough to orient a new user:

> Visual Day Planner

The distinctive promise is:

> See time. Shape the day.

## Positioning statement

For Android users with ADHD, time blindness, or a strongly visual way of thinking, Time To Do is a visual day planner that turns tasks into a malleable map of the day. Unlike list-first to-do apps and passive circular calendars, it makes overlaps, duration, and remaining time visible—and lets the user reshape today's plan directly without breaking the routine behind it.

## Audience

### Primary

Android-using professionals and students who identify with ADHD, time blindness, executive-function friction, or strongly visual thinking, and who:

- Plan a mixture of appointments, flexible tasks, and routines.
- Replan repeatedly as their day changes.
- Understand space, shape, and color faster than long lists.
- Feel that conventional calendars are precise but cumbersome and to-do lists ignore time.
- Want support without surrendering control to an automatic scheduler.

### Broader audience

- Freelancers and creators moving between different kinds of work.
- Caregivers coordinating personal and family time.
- Shift workers and people with strong daily routines.
- Any visual planner whose day changes faster than a conventional calendar is pleasant to edit.

## Founder-market credibility

The product comes from lived experience of ADHD, neurodivergence, and time blindness. That is valuable positioning evidence: the interaction is being built to solve a felt problem, not to decorate a generic planner with an ADHD label.

Use the founder story in concrete terms:

> “I built the planner my visual brain needed: one where I can see the shape of the whole day and move it when reality changes.”

Lead with the experience and resulting design decisions. Avoid implying that one neurodivergent experience represents everyone or that the app treats a medical condition.

## User problem

The schedule is usually represented in fragments: a list says what, a calendar says when, and the user's head must reconcile what fits. When plans shift, conventional tools make the user edit fields, reconsider dependencies, or scroll across the day.

The emotional problem is not just disorganization. It is losing a felt sense of the day and becoming unsure what is still realistic.

## Promise and proof

| Promise | Product proof |
| --- | --- |
| See the day as a whole | A bounded polar clock maps time, duration, sequence, and overlap spatially |
| Adjust without rebuilding | Drag and resize arcs with snapping, haptics, and undo |
| Let today be different | Occurrence-level edits preserve the recurring series |
| Recover quickly | Snooze, Extend, Do Now, Complete, and Skip actions |
| See patterns across days | Calendar views use miniature day rings and reports show recent behavior |
| Keep planning personal | Current local-first architecture requires no account or analytics |

## Differentiation hierarchy

1. **A live map of today.** The entire day is visible as one finite shape.
2. **Built for the change.** Moving, stretching, and recovering tasks is a primary interaction.
3. **Designed from time-blind experience.** Spatial cues and forgiving recovery reduce the mental translation conventional planners demand.
4. **Android-first.** The initial product should feel at home across Android phone, widget, notification, and eventually Wear OS surfaces.
5. **Flexible routines.** Today's exception does not have to destroy tomorrow's pattern.
6. **Calm and private.** The product supports attention rather than harvesting it.

The first two points must carry the story. The others make it credible and durable.

## Messaging hierarchy

### Short form

**See time. Shape the day.**

### Product descriptor

**Visual Day Planner**

For Android launch material, test the more specific descriptor:

**Visual planner for time-blind brains**

### Hero copy

> Turn today's tasks into a live 24-hour map. Spot overlaps, understand what fits, and move the plan as life moves.

Android/ADHD-forward alternative:

> A visual day planner for time-blind brains. See the whole day as arcs, then move it as life moves—built Android-first from lived ADHD experience.

### Three supporting messages

- **Your whole day, at a glance.** See when tasks happen, how long they take, and where they collide.
- **Plans move. Move with them.** Drag, resize, snooze, or start now without rebuilding the schedule.
- **Change today, keep the routine.** Adjust one occurrence while the recurring plan stays intact.

## Language to use

- Shape your day
- See what fits
- Move the plan
- A live map of today
- Your day, in the round
- Built for plans that change
- Made for time-blind brains
- Built Android-first
- Visual and spatial planning
- Private by design / stays on your device, when technically accurate

## Language to avoid

- “Revolutionary” or “the first circular planner.”
- Promises to cure ADHD, anxiety, time blindness, or other conditions.
- “AI-powered” unless automation becomes both real and central.
- Abstract mindfulness language that obscures the practical planner benefit.
- Productivity shame, hustle language, or messages that equate completion with worth.
- “Calendar replacement” before calendar integration and notification reliability support that claim.

## Positioning tensions to test

### Visual planner vs. ADHD planner

“Visual day planner” is broader and design-led. “ADHD planner” may acquire more efficiently but narrows the identity and raises the standard for accessibility and evidence. Recommended direction: explicitly lead with **time-blind brains** and the lived founder problem while keeping the category descriptor broad. Use “ADHD-friendly” in searchable and explanatory contexts, not as a promise of treatment.

### Android-first vs. Android-only

Android-first creates a sharp launch wedge and an authentic founder/platform story. Android-only would unnecessarily cap the longer-term market. Product and marketing language should celebrate Android quality without implying that the core idea belongs only to one operating system.

### Manual control vs. intelligent assistance

Direct manipulation is an advantage over opaque automatic scheduling. Future suggestions should feel like optional assistance—showing a possible move or identifying a conflict—not the machine taking over the plan.

### Zen calm vs. active utility

The identity can feel calm, but the copy must lead with concrete actions and outcomes. “Zen” is a design quality, not the product category.

## Open questions

- Does “live map” communicate better than “polar clock” to a first-time user?
- Is “Shape the day” understood as planning, or does it need the product descriptor beside it?
- Which pain resonates most strongly: overlap, time blindness, interrupted plans, or list fatigue?
- Should privacy be a lead differentiator or supporting reassurance?
- Does “for time-blind brains” feel welcoming and precise to neurodivergent users without excluding the broader visual-planning audience?
- Which Android-native surface creates the strongest differentiation after the phone app: widget, notification actions, or Wear OS?
